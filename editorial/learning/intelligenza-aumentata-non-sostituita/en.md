### Why this course matters

Giving AI subscriptions to an entire organisation without developing people's ability to judge outputs can produce two opposite but equally problematic reactions. Some employees avoid the technology, use it secretly or feel unable to engage. Others accept answers too readily, stop checking whether the work makes sense and pass plausible mistakes along to customers and colleagues.

Research on human interaction with automation describes **automation bias**: people may follow an automated recommendation despite contradictory evidence, or fail to notice a problem because the system did not flag it. The aim is not to reject automation or insist that every simple formatting task receive lengthy human reflection. It is to match human responsibility to **the costs of error, the need for judgment and the evidence available**.

### Learning outcomes and mission

Participants learn to distinguish tasks where AI removes mechanical friction from tasks where it may weaken essential judgment; identify workslop; apply pre-commitment, independent assessment and evidence-based verification; draft operational protocols for collaboration and review; and create role-specific AI literacy measures.

The mission is to shift attention from how quickly content appears on screen to the **quality of decisions and the total work required to deliver a reliable result**. Meaning, priorities, relationships and accountability remain human concerns even when tools help produce, analyse or organise information.

### AI literacy and the amended EU AI Act

Article 4 of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended by Regulation (EU) **2026/1744**, requires providers and deployers to **take measures to support AI literacy** for staff and other persons dealing with AI systems on their behalf, taking account of context and prior knowledge. It does not require a universal certificate for every person. This course's role-based matrix and assessment evidence are **MANZO AI educational recommendations**, not a mandatory template set by the law. [Official EU amendment](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj) · [European Commission AI literacy guidance](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers).

A single course does not by itself demonstrate regulatory compliance. Organisations must consider the systems, roles, risks and other legal or employment requirements relevant to their use.

## Reading 1 · The productivity illusion: what research says about workslop

Elena coordinates training in a 220-person service company. In September management assigned an AI writing assistant to nearly every employee. In November she receives two conflicting reports.

The sales director says: “We produce twice as many documents.” The administrative manager replies: “I receive ten-page reports that look polished, but the numbers do not add up, so I have to do them again.”

Both might be telling the truth about their part of the process. The disagreement reveals a measurement problem: **the person who generates a document may save time while transferring more work to whoever must use it**.

### What workslop means

The term *workslop* gained attention in September 2025 through research from BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab published in Harvard Business Review. It describes AI-generated material that **appears useful, complete or professional but lacks the substance needed to advance the work**.

A fluent ten-page report full of interchangeable management phrases and unverified statistics may be workslop even if its spelling is impeccable. The problem is not that AI was used; the problem is the mismatch between appearance and practical value.

### Read the numbers with care

A survey of **1,150 full-time US desk workers** reported that approximately **40%** had received workslop in the previous month. Respondents estimated that around **15%** of received work fell into this category, and the reported time required to interpret or repair each incident averaged **1 hour and 51 minutes**—often rounded to two hours. [BetterUp Labs and Stanford Social Media Lab](https://www.betterup.com/workslop).

These are **self-reported estimates from a US sample**. They are not direct timing observations in a controlled trial, do not prove that every employer experiences the same costs and cannot be automatically transferred to an Italian or European team. Wider cost estimates are extrapolations. They matter because they point to a real mechanism worth testing: the hidden cost of correction.

### Three warnings that a deliverable may be workslop

**Genericity.** A report would read the same for almost any organisation. It lacks actual constraints, decisions and relevant evidence.

**Claims without grounding.** Numerical projections, dates, legal references or purported studies appear without checkable sources or appropriate uncertainty.

**No actionable decision.** The document describes the topic but does not distinguish options, assumptions, risks, conditions or the next step.

Elena asks the sales team to measure not only how long a draft takes to generate, but also the recipient's review time, rework after delivery and errors discovered by customers. An impressive volume of text is **activity**, not evidence of valuable outcomes.

### Productivity must be measured at the end of the process

Suppose a salesperson previously spent 45 minutes preparing a brief that required 10 minutes of review. With AI, the draft takes 5 minutes, but the reviewer spends 70 minutes verifying and rewriting it. The total has increased from 55 to 75 minutes despite the apparent 40-minute saving for the writer.

That is a fictional calculation, not a published benchmark. The lesson is to measure the whole workflow, not the first output alone. The additional question is who bears that time and whether the result is more accurate, clearer and more useful.

**Micro-exercise.** Choose a recent AI-assisted document without confidential details. Estimate generation time, the receiver's review time, rework and errors found after release. Compare with a plausible non-AI baseline and label assumptions honestly.

**Reflection.** In your organisation, who pays for the correction of AI-generated work and who measures it?

## Reading 2 · Where AI helps and where it may weaken judgment: the task map

Elena suggests that management stop asking whether the company should “use AI” in general. The more useful question is **“Which parts of this particular task should be assisted, and which must be understood and decided by people?”**

### Two kinds of friction

**Mechanical friction** includes repetitive formatting, structuring a table of contents, generating alternatives, summarising material a person already understands and checking consistency of layout. AI can often reduce this effort, although quality checks may still be needed.

**Useful cognitive friction** includes defining the problem, deciding what evidence matters, judging competing values, resolving incomplete information and accepting responsibility for consequences. Removing all effort here may leave the person unable to tell whether the result is appropriate.

This distinction is a teaching heuristic, not a universal rule. For example, even formatting can carry high consequences if it changes decimal separators in financial data; conversely, brainstorming with AI can improve an expert's thinking when it is used deliberately.

### The two-axis task matrix

Elena evaluates tasks by **cost of error** and **need for professional judgment**.

| | Low judgment | High judgment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Low cost of error** | Delegate with light checks: formatting an internal draft | Collaborate: AI suggests structures or perspectives, person selects |
| **High cost of error** | Delegate with systematic verification: extracting contract numbers | Human-first: define criteria and assumptions, AI as challenger, human decision and documented review |

This matrix helps decide **how to collaborate**, not just whether the tool is allowed. A low-judgment extraction may still require independent numerical verification. A complex decision could benefit from AI-generated counterarguments, but the person must understand and own the final reasoning.

### Responsibilities that do not simply move to a model

**Relationships.** Difficult conversations, care, personal feedback and interviews require human sensitivity and accountable practice. AI may help prepare, but should not impersonate responsibility.

**Ethics and rights.** Decisions affecting employment, safety, access to essential services or personal rights require appropriate human and legal governance.

**Values and priorities.** An organisation must choose which outcomes matter and which trade-offs it is willing to accept.

**Final approval.** Whoever signs or sends work under their name must know what has been checked, particularly when the consequences are substantial.

This is not a claim that every action must remain completely manual. It is a design principle about **responsibility and judgment**, which can coexist with well-bounded automation.

### The central question before delegation

“If this output were wrong in a convincing way, **would I have the knowledge and evidence to detect it?**” A negative answer is a reason to obtain stronger expertise, independent checks or a different workflow—not simply a reason to write a longer prompt.

Elena notices that inexperienced workers can be least able to detect confident errors, precisely in tasks where they are tempted to delegate understanding. She suggests supporting them with reference documents, examples and structured review, not prohibiting them from learning with AI.

**Micro-exercise.** List ten recurring activities. Place each in the task matrix. For high-error-cost, high-judgment activities, write down what you will decide before opening the assistant.

**Reflection.** Which task are you delegating even though performing part of it is important for maintaining your competence?

## Reading 3 · Automation bias and blind delegation: what we know and do not know

Elena's administrative colleague notices a subtle change: “When the text is written confidently, I tend to believe it.” Psychology and human-factors research have studied similar effects long before current generative AI tools became common.

### Two related phenomena

**Automation bias** is a tendency to accept an automated recommendation despite contradictory information (a commission error), or to overlook a problem that an automated system did not mention (an omission error).

**Automation complacency** is a reduction in active monitoring because the system usually seems to perform well. High routine performance may encourage trust even when occasional errors are consequential.

Parasuraman and Manzey's 2010 review in *Human Factors*, **Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation: An Attentional Integration**, provides a relevant conceptual foundation. These concepts do not mean that all reliance on automation is irrational; they help us understand situations where scrutiny is mismatched to risk.

### Research on generative AI and knowledge workers

A study by Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon researchers, presented at **CHI 2025**, surveyed **319 knowledge workers** and collected **936 real-world examples of AI-assisted tasks**. It examined when people reported using critical thinking and how confidence related to their perceived effort.

The authors found associations between **greater trust in the tool and less self-reported critical-thinking effort**, whereas confidence in one's own ability to perform the task was linked to more reported critical-thinking activity. Participants described a shift toward verifying outputs, integrating answers and supervising AI. [CHI 2025 study](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778).

A separate paper by Gerlich in *Societies* (2025) discusses associations among AI use, cognitive offloading and critical thinking. [Gerlich](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/15/1/6).

### What the research does not prove

The cited studies rely substantially on **self-reporting and correlational designs**. They cannot demonstrate that generative AI causes a permanent decline in cognitive ability. They do not establish that every user becomes less intelligent or that cognitive offloading is always harmful.

External tools can support memory, calculation or information search without eliminating understanding. The risk varies with task, expertise, interface, incentives, feedback, training and oversight. The appropriate teaching goal is therefore **calibrated trust**, not blanket fear.

### Practical consequences for teams

People who know least about a task may have the weakest ability to detect plausible errors. Time pressure can discourage verification. Strongly polished prose may be mistaken for reliable reasoning. Conversely, well-supported expertise and access to sources can let people use a model as a useful challenge to their own judgment.

Elena proposes short exercises in which participants must identify a false but plausible claim. Instead of measuring whether they “liked AI,” the team measures whether they consulted evidence and changed a decision when facts contradicted an initial suggestion.

### A useful monitoring question

“How often did you verify the last five AI outputs you used, and by what method?” The answer is not a performance ranking of an individual. It is a starting point for understanding the workflow, creating better reference materials and making reviews feasible.

**Micro-exercise.** For five recent non-sensitive AI-assisted outputs, classify the kind of verification performed: source comparison, numerical check, peer review, independent calculation or none. Describe what you could change.

**Reflection.** On which tasks are you least able to judge a convincing error? Those require the most carefully designed support.

## Reading 4 · Human first, machine second: anchoring and positive friction

Elena's first question to the team is: “Before asking the assistant, did you already have your own view of the problem?” Many participants say no. They opened the tool and let its first answer define the categories and options.

### Anchoring: when the first answer shapes the rest

Tversky and Kahneman's 1974 work on judgment under uncertainty described **anchoring**: estimates can stay too close to an initial number or framing even when that starting point is arbitrary. With generative AI, a fluent first response may provide a powerful initial frame for subsequent work.

The danger is not merely believing a wrong fact. The first answer can determine **which options are even considered**, leaving out an important stakeholder, cost or ethical concern.

### The five-step “Human first” routine

1. **Define the problem.** Describe the actual decision in one sentence and list missing information.
2. **Write your hypothesis or criteria.** If you cannot yet propose an answer, record how you would recognise a satisfactory one.
3. **Identify risks and assumptions.** Distinguish what is known from what is guessed.
4. **Only then invite AI to challenge the plan.** Ask for alternatives or objections, not a replacement for your reasoning.
5. **Compare with evidence.** What did the model add? What did it miss? Which source must you check independently?

This approach is proportional. It is unnecessary to delay a simple, reversible formatting task for seven minutes. It is worthwhile for decisions with high error cost or a substantial need for judgment.

### Positive friction: purposeful checks

*Positive cognitive friction* means deliberately adding a brief pause that improves reasoning. Elena teaches five low-cost methods:

| Technique | What to do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Opposing case** | Build the strongest reasonable objection | “Which evidence would make this recommendation wrong?” |
| **Visible assumptions** | List unverified premises | “Show the assumptions in order of impact.” |
| **Primary-source comparison** | Check claims outside the model | “Which official documents should I independently consult?” |
| **Pre-mortem** | Assume the decision failed later | “What three factors would most likely explain failure in six months?” |
| **Reverse explanation** | Explain the answer yourself without rereading it | Write three lines defending the chosen conclusion |

A request such as “Criticise your own answer” can generate useful objections, but **the model's self-critique is not an independent verification**. The most valuable pause leads to checking evidence or revising a judgment.

### How to know whether the friction helped

Elena avoids rewarding participants for producing longer prompts. Instead, she looks for three things: the person revises at least one assumption where warranted; identifies information not independently checked; and can explain the final choice in their own words.

A decision should not be changed merely to prove that a learning method worked. A well-supported initial judgment may stand; what matters is whether alternatives and evidence were genuinely considered.

**Micro-exercise.** Choose an upcoming decision, avoiding personal or confidential details. Spend seven minutes writing your criteria, hypothesis and risks without AI. Use one of the five techniques, then note what actually changed.

**Reflection.** Which small moment of deliberate friction would be most helpful in your work, and where should it be inserted?

## Reading 5 · Role-based AI literacy and a disclosure-and-review protocol

Management asks Elena to demonstrate whether the AI programme improved actual competence. She replies that a single generic webinar is not enough for every role. An employee drafting routine emails, a developer building agents and a manager authorising consequential decisions face different risks.

### Three dimensions of learning design

A role-specific programme considers **job responsibilities**, **existing experience and education**, and **actual risk**: what information is processed, whether decisions affect people and what may be sent externally.

| Role | Demonstrable competence | Suitable evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Everyday user | Understand model limitations, avoid unapproved data uploads, identify hallucinations/workslop, verify outputs | Scenario quiz, repaired document and charter |
| Power user or workflow designer | Design repeatable steps, identify assumptions, test failures and document risks | Evaluated workflow and test cases |
| Reviewer or manager | Question claims, inspect sources, reject unsafe actions and manage escalation | Annotated review of a flawed deliverable |
| HR and learning | Design training matched to roles, record evidence and revise the programme | Role-based plan with measurable outcomes |
| Leadership | Set permitted uses and control levels, allocate review time and stop rewarding pure output speed | Documented boundaries, owners and decision criteria |

Elena asks for evidence of learning, not just attendance: corrected examples, explanations of errors, test scenarios and justified approvals. The programme should record date, subject, duration and responsible reviewer, then be updated when tools and risks change.

### A practical disclosure and review scale

Elena proposes an **internal governance protocol** with four levels. This is a MANZO AI teaching model, not a universal law requiring labels on every document.

| Level | AI contribution | Example | Internal action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **D0** | No substantial contribution | Spelling or formatting support | Usually no separate note under this example policy |
| **D1** | Preparatory assistance | Outline or alternatives | Note in working materials |
| **D2** | Substantial AI draft, reviewed by a person | Draft report or numerical table | State the contribution and reviewer |
| **D3** | External-facing or people-impacting work | Client communication, recruitment assessment, report to an authority | Document review, accountable approval and any applicable disclosure |

Actual contracts, professional rules, sectors and laws may require different disclosures or prohibit certain uses. The **Article 50** AI Act duties relate to specified interactions and categories of synthetic content, not all documents in an organisation. GDPR and confidentiality still govern the input and treatment of data.

### The human review note

For D2 or D3, a short review note can record **who checked, which factual and numerical claims were verified, which sources were consulted, which corrections were made and what remains uncertain**. The note is a record of professional review, not a guarantee that errors have disappeared.

For a numerical claim, the reviewer should be able to perform the calculation independently. For legal or medical claims, a prompt requesting sources is not enough: the underlying authoritative documents and competent review are needed.

### Measuring without surveillance

The organisation may wish to know whether teams check their work. However, detailed monitoring of individual employees' AI use has **privacy and employment-law implications**. Elena recommends aggregated indicators by process or team, transparent policies and appropriate legal/privacy consultation.

Suitable indicators include rework rate, review time, errors discovered after release, share of D2/D3 work accompanied by meaningful review evidence and practical learning results by role. She rejects rankings of individuals by number of prompts and simplistic claims about “hours saved” without a full workflow baseline.

**Micro-exercise.** For your role, write three competencies you should be able to demonstrate in a review and one real piece of evidence for each. Do not include sensitive employee information.

**Reflection.** If your organisation were asked how it supports AI literacy, could it show learning outcomes rather than only software purchase receipts?

## Reading 6 · Workshop: co-intelligence and anti-anchoring practice

**Duration:** 60 minutes. **Format:** individual work followed by optional peer review. **Materials:** fictional data only, no sensitive documents.

The workshop compares reasoning methods, repairs an attractive but flawed AI document and produces a **Human–AI Collaboration Charter** that can be tested in everyday work.

### Stage 1 · The anchoring exercise (15 minutes)

**Fictional problem.** A service business with 120 employees is choosing among three customer-support improvements: hire four new staff members, outsource evening coverage, or introduce a first-line AI assistant. Tickets have grown by 25% over a year, response time is worse, two important clients have complained, the budget is constrained and the IT manager is concerned about customer data.

Split into two experimental groups if a facilitator is available.

**Group A — Immediate delegation.** Ask an AI model to recommend a solution immediately, then review it.

**Group B — Human first.** Before opening any assistant, spend seven minutes defining decision criteria, risks and unanswered questions. Only then use AI to challenge your preliminary view.

Compare the proposed decision, evidence, missing information and ignored assumptions. Did Group A repeat the initial model framing? Did Group B identify different questions? A small classroom exercise with one case **cannot estimate causal effects or support statistical generalisation**. Its purpose is to observe reasoning strategies and generate hypotheses to test later.

On an on-demand platform, a learner can perform both procedures sequentially with the same fictional case, explicitly recording that this is a learning demonstration rather than a controlled experiment.

### Stage 2 · Repair the workslop (20 minutes)

Elena hands the participants a fictional AI-generated proposal:

> In today's rapidly digitising environment, AI represents an essential strategic opportunity. Recent studies demonstrate superior customer satisfaction wherever organisations invest in AI.
>
> Our analysis shows that the new assistant will reduce manually handled tickets by 30%, from 1,200 to 780 per month. Moreover, because the supplier is ISO/IEC 42001 certified, our company is automatically compliant with the EU AI Act and no further review of the data or process is needed.
>
> The company should therefore proceed immediately with full deployment, ensuring outstanding efficiency and customer experience.

The prose sounds polished, but it hides at least **two deliberate material errors** and several unsupported claims.

Apply a four-step repair method:

1. **Cut empty rhetoric.** Remove interchangeable phrases that contain no organisation-specific information.
2. **Isolate facts and numbers.** Mark each claim verified, awaiting verification or without a source.
3. **Restore missing context.** Identify budget, customer data, review capacity, IT integration, escalation paths and affected people.
4. **Rewrite the decision.** In less than half the space, state a proposed action, conditions, risks and next steps.

### Answer key for the deliberate errors

**Numerical contradiction.** Thirty per cent of 1,200 is 360, so a 30% reduction produces **840**, not 780. Moving from 1,200 to 780 means a 420-ticket reduction, or **35%**. The participant must not simply choose the more attractive figure: both the baseline and forecast need evidence.

**False legal inference.** Supplier certification under **ISO/IEC 42001** does not make the customer's specific use automatically compliant with the **EU AI Act**. Certification scope, legal duties, technical requirements and roles must be examined separately.

Other defects include unspecified “recent studies,” no documented trial, no cost calculation, no privacy analysis, no measurable acceptance conditions and no accountable reviewer.

### A defensible revised decision

An example would be: “Run an eight-week pilot on one low-risk support channel. AI drafts first-line responses with escalation to a human. Confirm the real ticket baseline and choose a defensible performance target; the 30%/35% claim has not been validated. Check data processing, applicable transparency and approval rules. Extend only after measuring error rate, escalation, review time, satisfaction and operational cost.”

This is still only an **illustrative proposal**, not a recommendation that every organisation should deploy an assistant.

### Stage 3 · Your Human–AI Collaboration Charter (25 minutes)

Write five concrete rules for your own role: two tasks where AI can produce first drafts under conditions; two tasks where you must define criteria or a hypothesis first; and one **non-negotiable final review** before signing, publishing or sending consequential material.

Then state what you never delegate, which categories of data you do not upload to unapproved tools and when the charter will be reviewed. In pairs, another participant should check whether each rule is **specific, observable, appropriate to the role and realistically usable**. Asynchronous learners can use the self-assessment rubric.

### Facilitator's schedule

| Minute | Activity | Deliverable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 | Introduce the task and the fictional-data rule | Safe setup |
| 5–20 | Compare immediate delegation and human-first methods | Qualitative observations |
| 20–40 | Workslop repair | Corrected claims and decision |
| 40–55 | Create the charter | Written personal protocol |
| 55–60 | Select one rule to apply next week | Concrete commitment |

### Rubric and hard STOP

Score 0–2 for: **(A)** independent criteria written before AI; **(B)** detection of two deliberate errors and unverified claims; **(C)** a compact, evidence-based rewritten decision; **(D)** concrete rules in the charter; **(E)** documented final human review.

**STOP regardless of total** if a consequential document has no final-review rule or the charter allows confidential data to be entered into an unapproved service. The score is a classroom assessment, not certification of competence or legal compliance.

**Final question.** Could you explain to a colleague which tasks you never delegate, what you always check and how you record your use of AI?

### 2. Four-step workslop repair card

**Cut:** remove generic language.  
**Isolate:** verify each number, date, legal claim and cited source.  
**Restore:** add the real context and constraints only the responsible person knows.  
**Rewrite:** provide decision, conditions, risks and next steps concisely.

### 3. Human review note for D2 and D3

| Field | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Document / task | ____ |
| AI contribution level | D2 or D3 |
| Tool and reviewer | ____ |
| Reviewed facts | Dates, figures, sources, legal or sector references |
| Sources consulted | ____ |
| Corrections and remaining uncertainty | ____ |
| Approval and date | ____ |

### 4. Process-level quality indicators

| Metric | What it may reveal | Caveat |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rework rate | Work transferred downstream | Define what counts as rework |
| Review time | Cost of validating AI material | Compare full-workflow baseline |
| Errors after release | Control failures reaching recipients | Record without blame culture |
| D2/D3 with substantive review notes | Application of the protocol | Quality matters more than presence |
| Practical learning outcomes | Actual competence by role | Assess tasks, not just attendance |
| Reported workslop | Whether people can raise quality problems | Reporting depends on psychological safety |

Do **not** use number of prompts, volume of generated text, unverified hours saved or rankings of individual AI activity as proof of value.

### 5. A 30/60/90-day implementation plan

**By day 30:** map tasks across a few important processes; identify roles and existing skills; choose a voluntary pilot team; review the disclosure protocol with privacy and legal advisers.  
**By day 60:** run scenario-based training by role; introduce the charter and D2/D3 review notes; collect initial workslop and error examples without identifying individual blame.  
**By day 90:** compare process-level indicators with a baseline; review the charter; record learning outcomes and decide whether to extend the programme.

### Reference glossary

**AI literacy:** knowledge and skills to use AI responsibly in context.  
**Automation bias:** incorrect deference to automated advice or omission of scrutiny.  
**Complacency:** lowered vigilance when a system usually seems reliable.  
**Workslop:** plausible AI-generated work lacking useful, verified substance.  
**Anchoring:** over-reliance on the first number or framing encountered.  
**Cognitive offloading:** assigning mental operations to external aids; sometimes useful, sometimes risky.  
**Positive cognitive friction:** a deliberate brief check that promotes understanding.  
**Pre-mortem:** imagining future failure to uncover possible causes.  
**Human review note:** record of what was checked, by whom, using which evidence.  
**Collaboration Charter:** role-specific rules about delegation, verification and responsibility.
