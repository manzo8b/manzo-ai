# AI Teaching Lab · Module 04

## An inclusive school where every learner can progress

*Adapt pathways, reduce barriers and turn assessment into guidance for improvement*

**Audience:** teachers, school coordinators and educational staff seeking responsible approaches to inclusive lesson design, formative feedback and AI-assisted drafting.

**Suggested workload:** around four hours for reading, two applied laboratories, revision and self-assessment. This is an estimate, not a validated delivery time.

**Expected artifact:** three equivalent ways for a fictional class to demonstrate the **same learning outcome**, one shared assessment rubric, feedback on invented work and a revised second attempt.

### Work independently and protect learners

Use only synthetic classroom examples. Do **not** upload actual pupils' names, grades, diagnoses, education plans, family data or identifiable work to unapproved AI tools. An assistant may help a teacher draft resources, but cannot decide an individual pupil's worth, needs, psychological state or formal grade.

Every workshop can be done on paper: define the outcome, plan several formats, write assessment criteria, review a fictional answer and explain how feedback supports the next attempt. The method remains useful without access to any model.

### The question guiding this module

Three students need to show they understand the causes of an environmental problem. They may use different modes of expression, but the school must be fair about **what is being assessed**.

How can teachers reduce barriers without assuming fixed learning styles, lowering expectations arbitrarily or asking an AI to make judgments about individual children?

## In-depth reading · three students, one competence

### 1. Different routes towards the same goal

A teacher prepares a lesson for a fictional lower-secondary class. The goal is that students can **explain two causes of local pollution and support each explanation with appropriate evidence**.

She initially assigns one long written paragraph with no structure, expecting every student to demonstrate the same understanding in the same form. Soon she sees that this format creates a barrier for some learners. One student can explain ideas orally but struggles to organise a long written response; another benefits from headings and a visual outline; a third likes writing unaided.

The teacher must not turn these observations into rigid labels or imaginary diagnoses. The problem is to identify the actual competence and decide which features of the activity are essential.

If the learning goal is *scientific argumentation*, a long unstructured paragraph may be one way to demonstrate it, but not the only way. The teacher proposes three alternatives:

- **Written explanation with an optional outline:** two causes, two evidence statements and a concluding relation.
- **Described concept map:** a structured map accompanied by a short explanation showing the reasoning, not merely keywords.
- **Short oral explanation with notes:** the same two causes and evidence described aloud, with a transcript or equivalent where needed.

The content requirements remain comparable: reasons, evidence and logical connections. The medium can change, subject to school policy and actual learner needs.

### 2. Designing to reduce barriers

**Universal Design for Learning (UDL)** is a framework that encourages planning different ways to engage with content, represent information and show learning. It is not a list of fixed student types. It does not claim that assigning everyone a permanent “visual”, “auditory” or “kinesthetic” category improves learning.

The teacher asks what might make the material hard to access: small text, an unfamiliar term, insufficient time, complex instructions, information delivered only through speech or an assessment that inadvertently measures handwriting instead of scientific reasoning.

She then considers flexible supports: a glossary, clear instructions, headings, a choice of accessible formats, sufficient time, a model of a completed response and an opportunity to revise after feedback.

These adjustments should **support the same defined competence** wherever that is pedagogically appropriate. In formal assessment, reasonable accommodations and rules must also follow applicable institutional procedures. It is not the role of a generic AI tool to decide which individual deserves an accommodation.

An educational assistant may propose alternative wordings or formats, but the teacher reviews for accuracy, inclusivity, feasibility and unintended changes in difficulty.

### 3. Feedback that creates a next step

A grade or praise can summarise an outcome, but formative feedback should help the learner understand **what worked, what remains unclear and what to try next**.

Compare two messages for a fictional student's work:

- “Good effort, but incomplete.”
- “You identified transport emissions as one cause. Add a second cause, quote evidence from the supplied source for both, and explain how each contributes to the problem. Then revise your conclusion.”

The second is observable and actionable. It relates directly to the rubric rather than evaluating the student's personality. It also makes another attempt possible.

A teacher may use AI to draft feedback wording from a **fictional sample** and an existing rubric, but should not allow a model to infer a pupil's health, identity, intelligence or permanent ability. Actual pupil work requires authorised processing and professional control.

The purpose is to improve learning. If feedback produces no opportunity for revision or response, part of its formative value is lost.

### 4. Metacognition needs concrete opportunities

Metacognition refers to thinking about one's own understanding and strategies. It is not developed merely by telling students to “reflect more.” A meaningful prompt might ask:

- Which part of your explanation is supported by a source?
- Which connection was hardest to justify?
- What did you change after the feedback?
- If you attempted the task again, what would you check first?

The teacher can make these questions part of a short **revision log**. Students compare their first draft with the revised version, identify an improvement and describe what evidence changed.

AI can help suggest questions, but the student should genuinely undertake the reflection. A fully generated “self-reflection” written without engagement defeats the purpose of the activity.

Feedback and metacognition reinforce one another when they point to a specific next action instead of a general impression of performance.

### 5. Dylan Wiliam and formative assessment

Dylan Wiliam's work on formative assessment supports the idea of collecting evidence of learning and using it to adapt instruction. A useful classroom question is not simply “Did everyone finish the task?” but **“What does this response reveal about understanding, and what teaching move follows?”**

The teacher designs a short exit ticket requiring each student to identify two causes, support one with a source and explain a relationship. The pattern of responses helps her decide whether to revisit the distinction between **cause, evidence and opinion**.

A model-generated worksheet can be part of the lesson, but the teacher's interpretation of the evidence remains important. In a high-stakes or regulated context, decisions about grading and student support cannot be delegated casually to an uncontrolled assistant.

The aim is **fair, comprehensible and useful feedback**, not an automatic label.

## The MANZO AI method as a working proposal

These seven steps are an internal project method, **not a scientifically validated general pedagogical standard**:

1. **Listen:** identify a concrete classroom barrier and learning difficulty.
2. **Classify the context:** subject, age, learning outcome and evidence of learning.
3. **Assess risks:** bias, unfair labels, disclosure of pupil data and inaccessible design.
4. **Ask targeted questions:** what information is missing and who needs to approve accommodations?
5. **Design:** create equivalent pathways, including an AI-free option and a shared rubric.
6. **Verify:** check that every option genuinely requires the same target reasoning, with proportionate accessibility supports.
7. **Deliver responsibly:** document the teacher's final decision and avoid automated personal judgments.

The labels are useful only when they lead to action. They are not intended as a checklist of technical commands to memorise.

## Workshop 1 · Three formats, one rubric

**Fictional setting:** a lower-secondary class must argue **two causes of an environmental problem** and support them with appropriate evidence.

**Task:** create three accessible assignment formats and a single scoring rubric that measures the target competence.

**Procedure**

1. State the observable outcome and three shared criteria **before** creating any media.
2. Draft a written version, a described concept-map version and an oral version with notes.
3. Check that each format requests two causes, evidence for each and a relationship explained in the learner's own words.
4. Identify any additional access supports that could reduce barriers without altering the core competence.
5. Use an authorised assistant with fictional data, if helpful, to identify ambiguities; do not ask it to assign diagnoses or grades.
6. Create a common rubric and explain it in plain language to learners.

### A sample shared rubric

Rate each dimension 0, 1 or 2, where a higher score requires clear evidence:

| Criterion | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Two relevant causes | Missing or unrelated | One well explained | Two clearly identified |
| Evidence | No relevant support | Partial or unclear support | Appropriate sources for both |
| Explanation | Statements without connection | Some causal explanation | Clear links and justified reasoning |

The form of expression does not add points by itself. A stylish diagram does not compensate for missing evidence; a lengthy essay does not guarantee clearer reasoning.

This rubric is an **educational illustration**, not an officially standardised assessment tool. Teachers may need more precise criteria according to the subject and school requirements.

### Portfolio and review

Save the three task descriptions, the common rubric and one paragraph explaining **how the options remain comparable**. Ask someone else to identify whether any format accidentally requires more or less disciplinary understanding than the others.

If one version asks only students to name two pollutants while another asks for a causal explanation and sources, the formats are not equivalent. Correct the task design before using it.

## Workshop 2 · From a mark to useful feedback

**Synthetic response:** “Pollution happens because there are cars. It is bad for nature.”

Do not grade a real child. Compare the fictional response with the criteria: one possible cause is mentioned but not explained with evidence; the second cause is missing; the causal mechanism remains vague.

**Procedure**

1. Underline information that is relevant and already present.
2. Identify what is missing against the common rubric.
3. Write two sentences of feedback addressed to the work, not the person.
4. Offer one concrete next attempt and a time or opportunity to revise.
5. Compare an initial and a revised **fictional** response to see whether the reasoning improved.

**Example of actionable feedback**

> You identified vehicles as a possible source of pollution. Explain how emissions from transport affect air quality using the supplied reference. Add a second cause supported by evidence, then rewrite your conclusion to connect both causes to the effect you are explaining.

Avoid “You are not capable of science” or “The algorithm says you need support.” Feedback must remain respectful, transparent and linked to the work.

### Second synthetic attempt

“Transport contributes to air pollution through emissions from engines. Industrial activities can also release pollutants. In the documents supplied by the teacher, each example connects an activity with a measured emission. Both can affect local air quality, though the effect depends on conditions.”

This revised text is still a **classroom illustration**, not a scientific report. A teacher should check the actual sources used in a real lesson. The aim is to make the improvement in explanation visible.

**Portfolio:** original fictional work, two feedback versions, a revised response and an annotation showing what improved.

## Integrated exercise · Personalise without labelling pupils

**Task:** build a lesson where different output modalities share one scientific objective and a common rubric.

1. Define the competence and three observable criteria.
2. Look for **barriers in materials and conditions**, not imaginary diagnoses.
3. Offer a written outline, a described concept map and an oral explanation with notes or an accessible transcript.
4. Apply the same rubric to a fictional artifact and formulate feedback for a second attempt.
5. Check whether the revised artifact shows an observable improvement rather than whether the learner “fits” an invented learning style.

**Example prompt**

> Help me prepare three accessible ways for a fictional lower-secondary class to explain two causes of pollution with evidence. Keep the same learning objective and assessment criteria across the formats. Do not assign diagnoses, learning-style labels or grades to students. Include an option that does not require AI and explain what I should verify before using the materials.

**Self-check:** What is responsible personalisation? The useful answer is **different accessible routes with common objectives and transparent criteria**, not labelling children “visual” or “auditory” for life, and not asking a generative system to decide their marks.

**Deliverable:** three equivalent pathways, one rubric and two versions of a fictional task demonstrating a revision.

## Vocabulary in context

**Inclusion:** designing conditions that allow meaningful participation without excluding people unnecessarily.  
**UDL:** Universal Design for Learning, a planning framework rather than fixed learner categories.  
**Accessibility:** ability to access and use content with varied devices and abilities.  
**Barrier:** feature of task or environment that prevents demonstrating the intended competence.  
**Accommodation:** adjustment made under relevant policies and individual needs.  
**Formative assessment:** using evidence to guide teaching and improvement.  
**Feedback:** actionable information about a particular task and possible next step.  
**Rubric:** explicit criteria describing levels of performance.  
**Metacognition:** reflection on one's own understanding and strategies.  
**Exit ticket:** short closing task producing evidence for the next lesson.  
**Evidence of learning:** observable performance tied to a goal.  
**Learning-style myth:** unsupported assumption that each person learns best when assigned a permanent modality label.

## Continue the learning journey

Once feedback becomes a guide for improving an attempt, a new possibility appears: an assistant that asks questions instead of merely giving the answer. Module 05 explores **Socratic tutoring, boundaries and human responsibility**.

**Bridge question:** Which questions should a tutor ask before revealing a solution, and when should it stop rather than guess about the learner?

## Formative verification and portfolio

Before opening the interactive self-check, explain:

- What is the difference between offering access supports and changing the learning objective?
- Why can a single transparent rubric evaluate different forms of expression?
- How does actionable feedback differ from praise or a mark alone?
- Why should a claim that an AI detector proves misconduct be treated cautiously?
- Which data and decisions remain outside an unapproved AI assistant's scope?

**Final artifact:** equivalent tasks, shared rubric, feedback and revision on synthetic learner work.
