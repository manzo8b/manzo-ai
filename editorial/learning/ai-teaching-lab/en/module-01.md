# AI Teaching Lab · Module 01

## Teaching in the age of artificial intelligence

*Understand AI, formulate professional requests and decide when a tool genuinely helps teaching*

**Audience:** teachers and school staff, including beginners in AI. No programming experience is necessary.  
**Estimated learning time:** about **four hours** for reading, two workshops, revision and formative evaluation. This is a planning estimate, not a required speaking time or a validated measure of effectiveness.

### How to work independently

Start with the classroom problem, not the assistant. Read the sections in sequence; when you reach a workshop, open a separate document with **Problem / Initial plan / AI or simulated response / Human revision**. You can complete every learning activity using the fictional examples provided, even without access to an AI service.

**Safeguard:** Never enter real pupils' names, marks, diagnoses, photographs, conversations, family situations or personal records into an unapproved AI service. Use exclusively synthetic information. Check your school's authorisation and policies before any real-world use. This open course does not generate an accredited certificate.

### This course demonstrates the method it teaches

The page itself illustrates a pedagogical decision. First there is a question; then a story, a possible investigation, an exercise, a reference, independent checking and a choice. The learner must do something with the result instead of merely reading a list of powerful AI products. Each module adds an artifact to a continuing professional portfolio.

We use the phrase **MANZO AI method** for our own design process. It is a project-specific operational proposal, **not** a validated general theory, official educational standard or compulsory procedure for schools.

### The question guiding this module

A teacher finishes the day with lesson plans, grading and materials still to prepare. An assistant can produce thirty slides in twenty seconds. **How can we tell whether those slides help students learn?**

At the end of this module you should be able to state an observable learning outcome, compare an AI-supported strategy with an alternative without AI, write an appropriately constrained prompt, check its result and justify a choice.

## In-depth reading · a classroom story that continues

### 1. The morning AI arrives in the lesson

Imagine a history teacher preparing a lesson on migration for a second-year secondary class. She already has a textbook, two short source extracts and a meaningful question: **How can students distinguish what a witness actually reports from what readers infer?**

A colleague suggests asking an AI tool to generate an entire lesson. The teacher could do that in seconds, but she first writes down what learning should look like. At the end of fifteen minutes, every student should be able to identify two statements directly supported by the fictional source extracts, separate one interpretation and explain why it is not a fact.

This goal is more useful than “learn about migration.” It creates a **visible performance**: a small comparison table with quotations, interpretations and evidence. When the teacher later asks an assistant to propose activities, she has a way to judge whether the output contributes to the objective.

Suppose the assistant proposes a polished slideshow about migration in general, complete with impressive statistics and colourful maps. Much of it may be irrelevant to this exact activity; some numbers may be unverifiable. It is not enough to be attractive. The teacher rejects the irrelevant parts and keeps only a brief source-comparison task that she can verify.

The first professional skill is therefore **problem framing**: deciding which student difficulty the tool should address. Software selection comes afterwards.

### 2. What AI and generative AI are

Artificial intelligence is a broad area of systems designed to perform tasks that involve capabilities we associate with cognition, perception, language or decision processes. **Generative AI** produces new text, images, audio, video or other material in response to inputs.

A language model learns patterns from large amounts of training material. It generates responses using the current context and its learned parameters; many modern products add retrieval, tools and safety layers. Its fluent explanations do not automatically guarantee that a historical claim is correct or that a source exists.

Teachers need a useful operational mental model, not myths about an intelligent person living in a computer. A model can be an excellent **drafting and comparison partner** and still fabricate a citation, misread an age-specific goal or overlook learners' needs. Neither excitement nor fear replaces a documented check.

It is also important to distinguish an actual generative assistant from a **deterministic interactive learning tool**. A branching exercise with buttons and prewritten feedback is interactive, but it is not automatically an adaptive generative tutor. Naming systems accurately helps students understand what they are using.

### 3. What changes for a teacher

AI may accelerate preliminary drafts, alternative examples, translation, summaries of authorised generic materials and initial multimedia planning. It can help an educator explore a topic from several angles. But the teacher still decides **what should be learned, what evidence counts, which sources are acceptable and how the result will be used**.

A frequent failure is to transfer too much cognitive work to the assistant. If the model chooses the lesson objective, the activity, the assessment and its own validation, the teacher has no independent basis for saying whether the proposal is good.

A stronger workflow starts with the subject, class level, expected performance and available resources. It compares the benefit of using AI with the time needed to verify, edit and explain the material. A short printed source sheet might outperform a multimedia lesson when the objective is close reading and time is limited.

**Cost matters too.** Free accounts can have usage limits, data-policy differences or changing features. An organisation should not build a mandatory exercise around an account that teachers or students cannot legally or practically access.

### 4. A prompt is a professional assignment, not a magic phrase

A vague request might say: “Make me a lesson on migration.”

A stronger **professional assignment** explains the real target and constraints:

> You are helping a secondary-school history teacher. Objective: students must distinguish verifiable statements from interpretations in two fictional short testimonies. Duration: 15 minutes. Materials: the two texts provided below; do not invent additional historical statistics. Propose one opening question, a paired comparison task and a short exit ticket. Show which student action demonstrates the objective. Mark assumptions and anything that requires a source check. Do not evaluate individual students.

A prompt is an instruction to prepare a **draft**, not proof that the draft is pedagogically valid. Its quality depends on the teacher's explicit goal, factual material, constraints, intended output and checks. “Act as the world's best teacher” cannot replace those elements.

When a tool returns an apparently complete answer, the educator should identify three kinds of content: useful material that fits the objective; material to fact-check; and material to remove or redesign.

### 5. From first draft to verifiable lesson

The teacher makes a comparison table with five columns: original learning outcome, AI draft, alignment, corrections and evidence of learning. She marks a generic motivational introduction as irrelevant; checks whether questions rely on provided text; and rewrites a final task so that students must **justify** a distinction rather than pick an answer at random.

She also plans an **AI-free route**. If the tool is unavailable, a teacher can still use the two fictional extracts and a paper worksheet. The lesson does not fail because a provider is offline.

A good material also needs to be usable. Colour alone should not encode the difference between facts and interpretations; labels should work for students with colour-vision differences. Long audio-only instructions should have a readable alternative. A complicated animated presentation may impose unnecessary cognitive load when students need to study two paragraphs carefully.

### 6. The pedagogical question comes before the technological answer

John Dewey's work on learning through inquiry helps us think about the educational value of purposeful questions, hypotheses and reflection. The practical lesson here is not to label every activity “Deweyan,” but to ask whether students will **investigate, explain and compare evidence**.

Constructive alignment similarly encourages teachers to connect intended learning, activity and assessment. If the goal is to distinguish facts from interpretation, the evidence must involve that distinction—not a satisfaction survey about how colourful the slideshow was.

An effective AI lesson is therefore not defined by the presence of AI. It is defined by a meaningful learning task, justified material choices, appropriate feedback and observable student understanding.

## The MANZO AI working method

The following seven steps are **an internal instructional-design aid**, not an externally validated scientific model:

1. **Listen and define the problem:** describe a real teaching difficulty before asking a chatbot for a solution.
2. **Classify the setting:** specify learners, discipline, outcome, context and evidence of learning.
3. **Assess risk:** identify possible errors, stereotypes, accessibility barriers, personal data and inappropriate automation.
4. **Ask focused questions:** list what is unknown and which permissions or references are needed.
5. **Design and construct:** compare a non-AI approach with an AI-assisted draft; write a bounded instruction if useful.
6. **Verify:** check sources, relevance, factual accuracy, duration, feasibility and accessibility.
7. **Deliver responsibly:** publish or use only a reviewed artifact with appropriate limitations and human ownership.

These steps can be adapted. You should be able to use them **even if you choose not to use AI**.

## Workshop 1 · From vague request to useful prompt

**Scenario:** a secondary class is studying two invented testimonies about migration. The goal is to distinguish facts stated in the texts from inferences and interpretations.

**Expected artifact:** a 15-minute micro-lesson, a revised prompt and a note explaining the changes.

**Instructions**

1. Write the observable outcome in one sentence before opening an AI tool.
2. Compare the vague request “Make a lesson about migration” with the professional prompt above.
3. Use an authorised assistant with fictional material, or analyse the simulated response below.
4. Highlight one step that fits the goal, one statement requiring verification and one part to redesign.
5. Produce a revised prompt and explain two specific changes.

**Simulated output:** “Begin with a ten-minute video on global migration trends, then tell the class that all witnesses describe migration in the same way. End with a quiz asking students to memorise five countries.”

**Quality review:** The opening video consumes most of the lesson; the generalisation about witnesses is unsupported; memorising countries does not show that students can separate fact from interpretation. The educator should replace the video with a short source comparison, remove the unsupported claim and use an evidence-based exit question.

**Suggested revised prompt**

> Draft a 15-minute source comparison using only these two fictional extracts. Students should underline two claims supported by text and one interpretation that cannot be established from it. Provide no invented statistics or personal details. Include an accessible print alternative and one formative question requiring a written justification. Label any assumptions.

**Portfolio:** original prompt, revised prompt, a corrected activity and one paragraph explaining why the second version supports learning more effectively.

## Workshop 2 · Save time without losing quality

**Scenario:** the same teacher must create two short assessment tasks and a review sheet before the next day.

**Instructions**

1. Prepare a non-AI baseline: how long does a simple task take using an existing lesson and the available texts?
2. Ask an authorised assistant for a first draft, using only fictional material and the documented outcome.
3. Record time spent drafting, correcting, checking sources and adapting for accessibility.
4. Evaluate the two methods on **total time**, not just generation speed, and on evidence of learning.
5. Identify one part of the task where AI genuinely helps and one that should remain under direct teacher control.

Use a table: **Method / Drafting time / Checking and editing / Total / Learning evidence / Data or accessibility risk**. This is a classroom comparison, not a statistically valid experiment.

A tool that generates six pages in a second but takes forty minutes to verify may not be a productivity improvement. Equally, an existing paper worksheet might be the superior solution for a quick task.

**Portfolio:** comparison table, a documented decision and an alternative if the service cannot be used.

## Integrated exercise · Choosing AI tools for teaching

**Professional challenge:** fifteen minutes in a secondary lesson to distinguish **fact from interpretation** using two invented witness accounts.

Compare four options: a general-purpose AI assistant, a document-grounded assistant, a multimedia editor and a printed worksheet with no AI.

| Criterion | Question to answer |
| --- | --- |
| Alignment | Which option actually leads students to distinguish claims and interpretations? |
| Reliability | Can you independently check the material and any sources? |
| Data | Does the tool require information that cannot lawfully be uploaded? |
| Accessibility | Can all learners access the activity and an equivalent alternative? |
| Time | What is the combined preparation and verification cost? |
| Price and availability | Can the school and students actually use the chosen tool? |

**Non-negotiable exclusion:** any service requiring identifiable pupil information without appropriate school authorisation is rejected, even if its design looks excellent. Do not average a critical data boundary with convenience or graphic appeal.

**A valid solution could be:** use the printed worksheet for the initial comparison, with the teacher optionally using an authorised assistant to develop two alternative prompts for discussion. The reason for this choice is not that paper is inherently better than AI, but that it may directly match the outcome with fewer dependencies.

**Deliverable:** a reasoned tool-selection matrix, at least one explicitly rejected option and a defined way to verify whether pupils learned the intended distinction.

**Check your reasoning:** a creative app requires uploading student work containing identifying information. Should you use it? **No, not with those data unless the organisation has confirmed that the processing is authorised and appropriate.** A good design cannot cancel a data-protection requirement.

## Professional vocabulary

**Learning objective:** what a learner should be able to demonstrate, not just a topic to cover.  
**Formative assessment:** evidence used to guide a learner's next step.  
**Prompt:** instruction and context given to a generative system.  
**Generative AI:** model capable of producing new content from input.  
**Scaffolding:** temporary support designed to build independent competence.  
**Constructive alignment:** consistency between learning outcomes, activities and assessment.  
**Synthetic data:** invented examples that do not identify real pupils or families.  
**Accessible material:** resource that provides usable alternatives for different abilities and devices.  
**AI literacy:** practical understanding of capabilities, limitations and responsible use.  
**Verification:** comparing consequential claims and materials with independent evidence.

**Before the quiz, explain:**

- Why is an observable student outcome needed before tool selection?
- Which task should a human teacher complete before allowing an assistant to draft a lesson?
- What makes a data-handling rule a non-negotiable exclusion rather than one more point in a score?
- How would you know that the activity still works without an AI service?

## Optional guidance for a facilitator

This course is written for **self-study first**. In group settings, do not replace the workshops with a lecture about products. Allow time for learners to produce and compare the original and revised draft, identify one important source check, explain one rejected tool and reflect on whether learning would still be possible without AI.

The four-hour time estimate includes independent preparation, reading, workshop and revision; it is not an instruction for a teacher to speak for four hours.
