# AI Teaching Lab · Module 02

## Critical thinking and reliable information

*Teach learners to distinguish a convincing response from verified knowledge*

**Audience:** teachers and school staff, including beginners in generative AI.  
**Estimated time:** four hours for reading, two workshops, a practical machine-learning exercise and revision. This is a design estimate, not a validated duration.

### Independent learning and safety

The learner works directly with the material: **read → predict → check → revise → explain**. Every example is fictional. Do not enter real pupils' names, work, marks, diagnoses, recordings or family circumstances into unapproved AI services. The tasks can be completed without any live model by studying the simulated examples.

Keep a professional portfolio document with four headings: **Claim / Evidence / Uncertainty / Revision**. The purpose of this module is to practise evidential reasoning, not to accuse students of misconduct or to score the performance of a particular AI brand.

### The question guiding Module 02

A student submits a polished text with neatly formatted references. The main argument sounds convincing, but one of the cited studies cannot be found. **How should a teacher investigate the problem without mistaking style for truth or suspicion for proof?**

By the end you should be able to verify a claim through an independent source, identify unsupported citations, guide learners through a correction process and distinguish training accuracy from generalisation.

## In-depth reading · a story that continues

### 1. The bibliography that looks perfect

A history teacher receives a student essay on nineteenth-century industrial change. It contains well-structured paragraphs, an elegant conclusion and references that appear academic. One citation is supposed to support an important statement about the dates of the first Industrial Revolution.

When the teacher searches the purported publication in a reliable catalogue, no such study can be found. The claim is also inconsistent with familiar historical chronology. The case is invented, but it illustrates a real educational problem: an apparently authoritative presentation may hide nonexistent sources and incorrect facts.

This issue is not entirely new. Students and adults were capable of making errors, misunderstanding sources or citing poorly long before generative AI became widely available. What changes is the ease with which convincing prose and plausible-looking bibliographic details can be produced.

The teacher should therefore avoid jumping from “This source may be invented” to “The student cheated.” Instead, she can ask the learner to show how the information was obtained, compare the reference with a real catalogue and revise the claim. The focus is on **evidence and learning**, not on technological accusation.

### 2. Why a language assistant can be confidently wrong

Generative models produce text shaped by training and context; fluency and factual support are different things. A model may generate a plausible title or author combination without access to a verifiable publication. It may also misread a document, conflate dates or overlook a question's limitations.

A fabricated quotation or citation is not corrected merely by writing “Be accurate” in a prompt. A teacher must verify that a cited work exists, that the relevant passage supports the specific statement and that no contradictory evidence has been ignored.

The difference between **finding a plausible answer** and **checking a claim** should be visible in the learning activity. A response such as “The Industrial Revolution began in 1925” is not acceptable simply because an AI tutor presented it with confidence.

Likewise, the absence of a link is not automatic proof of falsehood, and a link that exists is not proof that the quoted conclusion is true. Evidence involves content, provenance, context and interpretation.

### 3. Teach a repeatable verification method

The teacher gives the class a five-step procedure:

1. **Isolate the claim.** What exactly is being asserted? Separate dates, causal statements and interpretations.
2. **Find an independent source.** Prefer a traceable primary document, authoritative reference collection or reputable scholarly work appropriate to the question.
3. **Compare the wording.** Does the source genuinely establish the claim, merely discuss the topic, or contradict it?
4. **Record uncertainty.** “Not found in the consulted sources” is different from “proven false.”
5. **Revise and explain.** Rewrite the conclusion and show which evidence changed it.

This method remains useful whether the original text came from a human, an AI assistant or a combination. It also teaches learners that verification is a professional action, not an act of mistrust toward a person.

A classroom table might include **Claim / Source searched / Passage or finding / Judgment / Revised wording**. Each judgment should be open to reconsideration when new evidence is available.

### 4. Integrity is not visible from writing style

Some products claim to detect whether a text was generated by AI. Such detectors can produce false positives and false negatives; they should not be treated as infallible evidence of authorship or cheating.

A polished writing style is not a valid basis for punishing a learner. A teacher should rely on school procedures, transparent assignment expectations, process evidence, discussion with students and fair opportunities to demonstrate understanding.

A stronger assessment might include an annotated source log, a draft with revisions and a short oral explanation of a claim. These support learning and allow the teacher to evaluate disciplinary competence.

Students should also learn to document assistance honestly, according to the rules of their school. The aim is to develop academic integrity without turning every classroom into a surveillance environment.

### 5. Freire and learning to ask questions

Paulo Freire's work on dialogue and critical education is relevant as a lens for asking who speaks, what assumptions are taken for granted and why a claim deserves to be believed. This does not mean attaching the name of a thinker to a random exercise. It means giving learners genuine space to **question, justify, listen and revise**.

The teacher does not simply announce that the fabricated bibliography is wrong. She asks: “How could we establish whether the study exists? Which catalogue would you consult? What would make you change your conclusion?”

Students then compare different sources and explain their reasoning. The assessment evaluates **the quality of investigation**, not just agreement with the teacher or an AI answer.

The lesson also introduces a conceptual distinction: **truthfulness, source fidelity and usefulness**. A statement could be factually true yet irrelevant to the assigned question. Another might be useful as a hypothesis but still need evidence.

## The MANZO AI working method

The seven-step MANZO AI process is a **project proposal, not an official educational standard**:

1. **Listen:** identify the learner's real difficulty.
2. **Classify context:** subject, age, learning outcome, evidence and available references.
3. **Evaluate risks:** misinformation, invented citations, bias, privacy and unfair disciplinary consequences.
4. **Ask targeted questions:** what do we need to know, and what evidence would count?
5. **Design:** prepare a verification activity, with and without AI.
6. **Verify:** compare sources, reasons, time, accessibility and effect on learning.
7. **Deliver:** document the investigation and the teacher's final, accountable decision.

The method supports **understand → apply → evaluate → create**. Memorising these labels is less important than demonstrating a defensible action at every stage.

## Workshop 1 · The essay with an invented reference

**Situation:** a completely fictional essay states, “The first Industrial Revolution began in 1925,” and cites a non-existent study.

**Objective:** learn to verify factual claims without accusing the author of misconduct.

**Procedure**

1. Underline two distinct claims: the date and the purported evidence.
2. Write what a trustworthy history source would have to show for each claim to stand.
3. Ask an authorised assistant to suggest a **verification checklist**, not to declare itself the final authority; alternatively use the checklist above.
4. Check at least one accessible external source yourself.
5. Rewrite the flawed paragraph and design a question requiring a learner to explain how the evidence was found.

**Example of a poor AI response:** “The year 1925 is correct because authoritative historians agree.” This provides no verifiable authority and repeats the contradiction.

**Example of a useful correction:** “The claim should be revised after consulting reliable historical references. The first Industrial Revolution is generally associated with developments beginning in eighteenth-century Britain; precise periodisation varies by author and region. Cite the particular source consulted rather than an invented study.”

**Final deliverable:** an annotated claim table, a corrected paragraph, a note on uncertainty and a fair classroom verification activity.

### How to assess the workshop

A good result can distinguish “I could not find this publication” from “This publication definitely does not exist.” It makes clear what the independently consulted source supports. It does not infer student misconduct from text style alone.

If learners cannot explain what they checked, the activity needs another round; a fluent final answer is not sufficient.

## Workshop 2 · The sensational message in a class chat

**Situation:** a fictional message circulating in a class claims that a new rule will cancel all examinations next week. It includes a screenshot without a date, issuing authority or verifiable link.

**Task:** prepare a ten-minute source-verification activity.

1. Ask learners to describe the claim without forwarding the screenshot to other groups.
2. Record which authority would be competent to announce such a measure.
3. Consult an official, current channel appropriate to the educational setting, or work with a clearly fictional official notice supplied by the instructor.
4. Mark what is known, missing and contradicted.
5. Prepare a neutral correction that avoids shaming classmates and encourages checking before sharing.

Do not ask pupils to reveal their personal group messages or forward real private communications. The synthetic screenshot can be created in a worksheet or described in text.

**Deliverable:** a reusable fact-checking sheet with question, source, date, conclusion and review. Include an accessible text-only version.

**Reflection:** how can a teacher teach scepticism without implying that every surprising claim is necessarily false?

## Integrated exercise · Inside machine learning: train and test a rule

A small synthetic dataset contains **four blue triangles and four red circles**, all labelled by shape. Consider two rules that both appear correct on this **training sample**:

- “If it is blue, classify it as a triangle.”
- “If it has three sides, classify it as a triangle.”

Now test both rules on four **new** cards: a red triangle, a blue circle, a blue triangle and a red circle.

| Test item | Colour shortcut | Shape-based rule | Correct shape |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Red triangle | Circle | Triangle | Triangle |
| Blue circle | Triangle | Circle | Circle |
| Blue triangle | Triangle | Triangle | Triangle |
| Red circle | Circle | Circle | Circle |

The colour shortcut looked perfect on training examples because colour and shape were artificially correlated. On the held-out cards it fails twice. The shape-based rule continues to match the intended label.

This is a simplified, manually specified classification experiment—not the training of a real neural network. It illustrates the difference between **training performance** and **generalisation to new combinations**, and why dataset shortcuts can be misleading.

**Sequence**

1. Describe the training examples and target labels.
2. Explain why both rules seem to fit the initial data.
3. Apply each rule to unseen cards **before** looking at the answer table.
4. Count the mistakes and show why an apparently good training accuracy is insufficient.
5. Design an additional test card that would help reveal a different shortcut.

**Optional prompt**

> Help me explain a fictional classification task with blue triangles and red circles. Do not assume that a high training score guarantees generalisation. Create a test table with unseen combinations, identify possible shortcuts and ask students to justify their predictions before revealing the expected labels.

**Deliverable:** training/test table, predicted outcomes, error counts and a reflection on data distribution.

**Check:** a learner should be able to explain why the blue-colour rule fails for a blue circle without simply saying “the algorithm is stupid.”

## Professional vocabulary

**Hallucination:** generated information that is unsupported or false within a task's criteria.  
**Claim:** specific statement that can be checked against evidence.  
**Primary source:** original record or direct evidence relevant to a question.  
**Secondary source:** interpretation or analysis based on other evidence.  
**Source fidelity:** degree to which a summary or answer matches a source.  
**Training data:** examples used to learn or configure a model.  
**Test data:** separate examples used to assess generalisation.  
**Label:** expected category or answer attached to an example.  
**Shortcut learning:** using an easy but unreliable correlation rather than the target relationship.  
**Generalisation:** appropriate performance on cases not identical to the training examples.  
**Automation bias:** accepting automatic suggestions despite contrary evidence.  
**Critical literacy:** capacity to interrogate information, sources and assumptions.

## Formative verification

The interactive course provides a multiple-choice scenario, explanation, source-matching task and a small decision pathway. These are **formative browser-only activities**, not a private examination or automated evaluation of real pupils.

Before reviewing answers, explain:

- Why does a fabricated citation demand a source check rather than immediate accusation?
- What does the coloured-shape exercise illustrate about unseen data?
- Why is an AI-authorship detector alone inadequate as evidence of misconduct?
- What does a useful correction include besides the phrase “This is false”?

**Portfolio:** an evidence-based correction sheet and a training-versus-test exercise.

## Optional facilitator guidance

Use invented material. Let learners form a provisional conclusion before showing corrections; offer respectful feedback that distinguishes weak evidence from dishonest intent. Encourage source checks outside the model. The workshop is not an experiment proving that any particular AI system is better or worse than students.
