# AI Teaching Lab · Module 03

## Designing materials that actually help people learn

*From slides to podcasts: create clear, accurate and accessible multimedia learning resources*

**Audience:** educators and school staff who want to design multimedia resources with or without AI. Programming and paid subscriptions are not prerequisites.

**Estimated effort:** around four hours for reading, planning, workshops, iterative revision and formative evaluation. This is an educational estimate, not a tested guarantee.

**Professional product:** a small, reusable **multimedia learning kit** built from one observable objective, with a storyboard, concise slide or visual, a short audio script, a text equivalent, source records and a documented quality check.

### How to study this module independently

A great-looking image or slideshow does not automatically support learning. Keep the learning objective visible throughout this unit. Work with **fictional content**, public-domain or appropriately licensed resources and authorised software. Do not upload real pupils' pictures, voices, grades, health information or recordings to unapproved external AI tools.

You can create a storyboard, slide outline and podcast transcript entirely on paper or in an ordinary text editor. A generative model can assist, but the method is not dependent on having an account with a particular provider.

### The question guiding the module

A teacher has forty slides full of visually impressive charts. Students remember the colours but cannot explain the underlying idea. **What should be removed, redesigned or verified so the material makes learning possible?**

By the end, you should be able to choose the right representation for a teaching goal, identify cognitive overload, select tools by output and risk, build an accessible mini-kit and test whether a learner can use it.

## In-depth reading · the story of a beautiful but misleading graph

### 1. When beauty gets in the way of explanation

A teacher prepares a presentation about climate change for a secondary classroom. The AI-generated slides look remarkable: rich textures, gradients, attractive icons and an animated line chart. During the lesson, students notice the colours and motion. When the teacher asks what the graph demonstrates, answers differ wildly.

One learner says that the temperature has doubled; another believes a huge spike occurred within one year. The graph's axis is cropped and the units are difficult to read. The material is not simply decorative: **its visual decisions change the conclusion students are likely to draw**.

The teacher checks the source data and realises that the graph needs a title expressing the measured phenomenon, labelled axes, a defensible scale, a stated period, a citation and a written explanation of limitations. She also has to decide whether a graph is needed at all.

A visually beautiful resource may still be educationally defective when it hides the evidence, directs attention to irrelevant details or encourages an unsupported interpretation.

### 2. Cognitive load and limited attention

Human working memory has constraints. When an explanation requires simultaneous attention to long paragraphs, moving animations, tiny chart labels and background audio, some of the learner's effort is spent **managing presentation rather than understanding the concept**.

The work of Richard Mayer on multimedia learning is an important reference for principles such as coherence, signalling, spatial and temporal contiguity, segmenting and avoiding irrelevant elements. These are guides informed by research, not simplistic rules that every animation is bad.

**Coherence:** remove elements that do not serve the learning objective.  
**Signalling:** make the key relationship noticeable through a heading, pointer or emphasis.  
**Segmenting:** divide a complex explanation into meaningful steps rather than demanding that everything be understood at once.  
**Modality choice:** use words, diagrams, audio and video according to what they help the learner do.

The teacher redesigns the chart as a single understandable visual with a short explanatory sentence, a source link and an optional more detailed table. She keeps separate screens for distinct concepts instead of shrinking ten charts into one slide.

The result is less spectacular but more informative. Good multimedia design is not measured by the number of simultaneous effects.

### 3. Accessibility makes the learning decision more explicit

Accessibility must be designed before publication. A resource that relies on red and green colours alone to distinguish categories will exclude some learners. A podcast without a transcript is unavailable to someone who cannot hear the recording or wishes to search for a definition later.

For each element, consider:

- **Image:** meaningful alternative text describing the information, not merely the visual style.
- **Chart:** labelled units, a plain-language summary and a data table or equivalent where useful.
- **Video:** accurate captions and an alternative where visual-only information is necessary.
- **Audio:** a readable transcript and clear pacing.
- **Interactive activity:** keyboard operation, visible focus, labels and feedback not dependent on colour alone.
- **Document:** logical headings, reading order and meaningful link text.

Accessibility can improve the design for everyone by requiring precise communication. It does not mean replacing every experience with an identical text wall; it means providing **equivalent access to the actual learning goal**.

A teacher should test resources on a phone, on a small screen, at increased text size and with keyboard controls when relevant. A design that only works during a polished projector demonstration is insufficient.

### 4. The educator's review is a creative task

An AI-generated illustration may be visually appealing but scientifically imprecise. It may add organs that do not exist, distort map boundaries or place misleading labels on a diagram. A voice generator may pronounce technical terminology incorrectly. A video editor may put a subtitle at the wrong moment.

The educator must decide what the material claims and what a learner is likely to infer. Factual checking should be performed **against authoritative subject sources**, not by asking the same model to approve its own output.

Review is also a design act. The teacher may replace an animated globe with a two-column comparison, remove decorative footage, change examples for the class context or choose a non-AI diagram because it is easier to verify.

A strong review asks: Which statement is supported? Which representation matches the objective? Which element introduces a potential misconception? Which alternative would allow a learner to explain rather than merely watch?

### 5. Papert and learning through construction

Seymour Papert's constructionist perspective emphasises the value of learners building, testing and revising meaningful artifacts. In a multimedia classroom, this could involve students making an explanatory diagram, recording a short narrative or comparing several ways to represent the same phenomenon.

The important element is **the reasoning visible in the artifact and its revisions**, not the technical sophistication of the production tool. A simple self-made concept map may show more understanding than an instantly generated film that no student can explain.

The educator's role is to set a meaningful question, provide constraints and feedback, encourage experimentation and require justification. AI may remove some mechanical barriers, but it should not erase the learner's opportunity to decide what the message means.

## Designing multimedia content: choose the medium after the question

An effective plan requires **six decisions before opening a media-generation interface**:

1. **Learning outcome:** What observable competence should improve? Avoid “Make an exciting video” as the whole objective.
2. **Audience and prerequisites:** What do the learners already know, and which vocabulary or representations might be difficult?
3. **Evidence and sources:** Which facts are essential, who supplied them and how will they be verified?
4. **Representation:** Should the answer be a photograph, map, diagram, audio explanation, short video, text or activity?
5. **Accessibility and ethics:** What equivalent format is needed? Is the content licensed and safe to process?
6. **Evaluation and revision:** What must learners explain or do, and what would cause the material to be rejected?

Write these decisions in a **media-choice matrix**. If a learner needs to distinguish two causal mechanisms, a labelled comparison diagram might be better than a cinematic montage. If the goal is pronunciation practice, audio could be appropriate; it still needs a textual companion.

### Selecting a generative model by output

Product names and feature lists change. Teach the choice **agnostically**, then use an updateable tool sheet.

| Desired result | Model or tool family | Quality checks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fact-based lesson outline | Language model with authoritative source access | Verifiable citations, context, age fit, no unsupported claims |
| Explanatory image or illustration | Image-generation tool or diagram editor | Factual accuracy, no misleading text, licensing, alternative text |
| Short narrated audio | Text-to-speech or audio editor | Pronunciation, script fidelity, transcript, voice rights |
| Demonstration video | Video editor or generative video system | Sequence accuracy, consent/licensing, captions, human review |
| Small interactive quiz | Authoring software or rule-based web interface | Correctness of answers, keyboard use, accessible feedback |
| Data chart | Spreadsheet and chart tools | Data provenance, axis scale, units, honest representation |

A tool should never be selected merely because it produces a spectacular sample. Choose the simplest system that can satisfy the objective, even if it uses no AI at all. Record cost limits, storage location, privacy conditions and the option to work offline or on paper.

## Workshop 1 · From forty slides to a climate micro-kit

**Fictional starting situation:** a teacher inherited a forty-slide presentation on climate. It includes unsupported numbers, long paragraphs, decorative stock images and a misleading chart.

**Goal:** produce a compact learning kit for a fifteen-minute session where students can explain the difference between an observed measurement, an interpretation and a proposed action.

**Procedure**

1. Write the desired learner explanation in one sentence.
2. Select no more than **three essential claims**. Mark which require authoritative climate or science sources before publication.
3. Remove repeated introductions, decorative elements and information unrelated to the goal.
4. Draw a two- or three-screen storyboard: opening question, evidence, student response.
5. Redesign the chart with meaningful units, labelled period and honest scale. If data have not been checked, mark them **synthetic** and do not present them as actual climate observations.
6. Write accessible alternative text for the visual and a short textual data description.
7. Create a final two-question exit ticket requiring an explanation, not simply recall of chart colours.

**Example synthetic outcome:** “Question: What can we conclude from a change over time? Screen 1 states the measured variable and dates; Screen 2 distinguishes observations from hypotheses; Screen 3 asks students to explain which conclusion is supported and which would require more evidence.”

The teacher deliberately avoids claiming a specific real-world temperature trend unless the source is cited and the graph has been checked.

**Deliverable:** a one-page media plan, a redesigned visual or paper sketch, alternative text and two exit questions.

### Check the product

Ask whether the chart can be understood without its colours, whether axes and dates are readable at normal and enlarged sizes, whether the text matches the source, and whether a student can state **what the evidence shows and what it does not show**.

A complete set of slides is not proof of learning. The artifact must be inspected from the learner's point of view.

## Workshop 2 · A short citizenship podcast

**Fictional situation:** a secondary class is exploring how public rules, rights and responsibilities affect daily decisions. The educator proposes a two-minute educational podcast to introduce the discussion.

**Goal:** students should be able to identify one claim, ask for its source and compare it with an alternative perspective.

**Procedure**

1. Write a 150–250-word outline with an opening question, two evidence-based points and a final reflection.
2. Mark every legal or institutional claim that requires verification before recording. Use fictional scenarios if the applicable law is not being checked.
3. Choose whether the voice should be recorded by a person, produced with authorised text-to-speech or left as a reading script. Avoid copying a real person's voice without consent.
4. Record or simulate the narration. Keep music and sound effects subordinate to the explanation.
5. Write and check the **complete transcript**, including meaningful non-speech cues if they carry information.
6. Prepare a paper alternative containing the same core question, claims and reflection.
7. Ask a learner to explain the key distinction without listening again.

**Simulated excerpt:** “A neighbourhood group shares a message about a new rule. Before forwarding it, which source could confirm that the rule exists? Why might two people interpret the same announcement differently?”

This is deliberately a question rather than a claim that a particular law exists. The teacher chooses an official source and adds a citation before using a real-world example.

**Deliverable:** script, two-minute audio or simulated storyboard, full transcript, source list and two questions for formative discussion.

### The difference between creation and publication

Drafting does not mean a resource is safe to share. The educator reviews accuracy, permissions, accessibility and suitability for the learners. An AI-generated voice, image or video cannot be treated as free from intellectual-property and consent issues merely because it is synthetic.

## Production laboratory · Build and export your accessible kit

Create a **mini media kit** with five elements:

- A project brief that names the objective, audience and evidence of learning.
- One original explanatory visual or a clearly labelled paper storyboard.
- A short narration script or audio file, with a complete transcript.
- Two interaction or reflection questions requiring reasoning.
- A quality checklist with source, accessibility, licensing and human-approval status.

If you use a free design platform, confirm that the export formats and licences meet your needs. If a technical tool is unavailable, package the same components in Markdown or a standard document. Do not invent a downloadable file or pretend that an audio/video has been generated unless it actually exists.

### A professional prompt you can adapt

> Help me design an accessible multimedia learning kit for a fictional secondary class. The learning outcome is [observable action]. I will supply the verified source material. First compare a static visual, a narrated audio explanation and a non-AI worksheet. For each, explain how learners demonstrate understanding, accessibility requirements, verification risks, time and cost. Do not invent scientific facts or student data. After my choice, draft a concise storyboard, a script, alternative text and an assessment question. Mark everything that requires independent source review.

The prompt is a planning aid, not a substitute for subject expertise.

### Minimum quality rubric

Score each dimension 0, 1 or 2 with a written reason: **goal alignment**, **factual accuracy and source fidelity**, **accessibility and equivalent format**, **appropriate cognitive load**, and **observable learning evidence**. A serious factual, privacy or consent failure creates **STOP regardless of the total**.

If learners cannot explain the material or the teacher cannot verify its main claim, the kit is not ready for classroom use.

## Professional glossary

**Multimedia learning:** purposeful combination of representations to help understanding.  
**Cognitive load:** demands placed on working memory during a learning task.  
**Coherence principle:** avoid irrelevant material that competes with essential information.  
**Signalling:** emphasise the structure or key information of a presentation.  
**Segmenting:** divide complex content into meaningful units.  
**Alternative text:** textual description serving the informational role of an image.  
**Transcript:** readable form of audio speech and relevant sounds.  
**Captions:** text synchronised with speech and meaningful audio in video.  
**Storyboard:** ordered visual plan of scenes or screens before production.  
**Source provenance:** origin and evidence supporting the content.  
**UDL:** Universal Design for Learning; framework for planning access and participation.  
**Constructionism:** perspective emphasising learning by building meaningful artifacts.  
**Formative assessment:** feedback that informs the learner's next step.  
**Media-choice matrix:** documented comparison of representations, tools, cost and risks.

## Continue the learning journey

The next module addresses **inclusive design and formative feedback**. Multimedia can remove barriers only when its goals and measures are clear. Students may need different ways to express the same competence, without being assigned fixed “learning styles” or personal diagnoses.

**Bridge question:** how can one learning outcome be demonstrated in several accessible ways while keeping assessment criteria fair?

## Formative verification and portfolio

Before consulting the interactive feedback, explain:

1. Why is a beautiful chart potentially misleading?
2. What is the purpose of a transcript and equivalent text alternative?
3. Why should the media choice be made after the learning outcome?
4. Which source or legal limitation could block publication of an otherwise attractive asset?
5. How would a student demonstrate understanding after using the mini-kit?

**Module artifact:** accessible multimedia learning kit, storyboard, source record, transcript and documented revision. No real student data or school records should be used in the learning demo.
